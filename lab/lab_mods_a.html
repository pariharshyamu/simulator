SIM-1 · Monitoring & maintenance

Residual anomaly detection — ID fan bearing

Why a model that knows what the bearing should be running at finds a fault weeks before a fixed threshold does — and what destroys that advantage.

The problem. A DCS alarm fires at a fixed number. That number has to be set high enough not to nuisance-trip at full load on a May afternoon, which makes it blind at forty per cent load on a January night. By the time it operates, most of the P–F interval is gone. Availability shortfall cost MAHAGENCO ₹100.87 crore of disallowed fixed charge; a large part of that is unplanned.

SIM-2 · Performance & efficiency

Heat rate loss attribution

The June 2026 net heat-rate gap was worth ₹56 crore in one month. This module splits that gap into the part that belongs to the boiler and turbine and the part that belongs to the auxiliary switchgear — and shows that the answer is completely different at different stations.

The identity everything rests on. Generated (gross) heat rate = net heat rate × (1 − auxiliary fraction). Rearranged, it tells you whether a station's heat-rate penalty is a combustion problem or an electrical one. Nashik's boiler and turbine are beating the norm; its entire penalty is auxiliary power. Koradi Units 8-10 are the opposite.

SIM-3 · Performance & efficiency

Auxiliary power optimiser

MAHAGENCO burned 81.5 million units above normative auxiliary consumption in June 2026 — worth about ₹33 crore at each station's own variable charge. Much of it is a scheduling problem, not an equipment problem.

The problem. How many mills, how many CW pumps, which fan control mode, how many ESP fields? On most shifts the answer is "whatever the last shift left running". Every combination has a different auxiliary power draw and a different effect on heat rate — more CW pumps cost auxiliary power but improve back pressure. It is a small optimisation problem that a computer solves exactly and habit solves badly.

Where this is simpler than your plant. Real constraints include mill maintenance schedules, coal quality varying between bunkers, CW pump changeover restrictions, ESP hopper heater loads, ash handling cycles, and the fact that a mill start is not free. This model assumes changeovers are costless and instantaneous. They are not — a real optimiser must include switching cost or it will chatter.
SIM-4 · Fuel

Coal GCV soft sensor

As-fired GCV across MAHAGENCO's stations ranged from 2,733 to 3,270 kcal/kg in June 2026, and between 619 and 941 kcal/kg was lost between the loading and unloading ends. The lab result arrives after the shift has already made its decisions. A soft sensor fills the gap.

The problem. You cannot control what you learn about a day late. A soft sensor is a model trained on the lab results you already have, which then estimates as-fired GCV every minute from signals the plant already measures. It does not replace the laboratory — it interpolates between laboratory results.

What just happened

Where this is simpler than your plant. Real coal changes source, and a model trained on one supply will mislead you on another — this is the single biggest failure mode of a GCV soft sensor. Real sampling has its own bias. Real mills wear, which changes the relationship the model learned. Any deployed soft sensor needs a standing comparison against the laboratory and a retraining trigger when the source changes.
